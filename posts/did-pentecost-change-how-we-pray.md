## A Question Nobody Asked Me in Sunday School

Here's a thought that landed on me recently and wouldn't leave: when Jesus taught the disciples to pray, the Holy Spirit hadn't come yet.

Think about that. The Lord's Prayer — the most famous prayer instruction in history — was given to people who did not yet have the Comforter living inside them. The Spirit came later, at Pentecost, after Jesus ascended.

So the question practically asks itself: does the Spirit's arrival change how we're supposed to pray? If the Helper now dwells *within* us — if He's our Comforter, resident and not visiting — shouldn't that reshape the whole mechanics of the thing?

I went digging, and the answer surprised me with how clear it is. Yes. Something fundamental shifts at Pentecost — not the content of prayer, but its entire direction of travel.

## Before: Praying Toward a God You Approach

Go back to Luke 11, where Jesus teaches the disciples to pray. He gives them the Lord's Prayer, then the parable of the friend knocking at midnight — and then a climax most of us read right past:

"If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give *the Holy Spirit* to those who ask him!" (v. 13)

Look at the structure of that. Jesus is teaching them to ask *for* the Holy Spirit. They don't have Him yet. The pinnacle gift the Father wants to give isn't circumstantial relief, a better job, or a healed situation — it's the Spirit Himself. The entire prayer lesson in Luke 11 is quietly pointing forward to Pentecost as its own answer.

And notice the opening address: "Our Father *in heaven*." Prayer before Pentecost is external approach. You come toward God. You petition a Father who is gloriously real but located, in the language of the prayer itself, somewhere you are not.

That's not a defect — it's the stage of the story they were in. Jesus prayed the same way for them: His great prayer in John 17 is intercession *for* the disciples, offered on their behalf, from outside them. They were covered by prayer. They were not yet inhabited by the One they were praying to.

## After: Praying From Within a God-Indwelt Life

Then Pentecost happens. The Spirit falls, Joel's prophecy is fulfilled — "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" — three thousand people are baptised in a day, and the first church settles into its rhythm: the apostles' teaching, the fellowship, the breaking of bread, *the prayers*.

The geography of prayer has changed. The believer is no longer approaching a God who is only external. The God they pray *to* now dwells *in* them. Prayer stops being a long-distance call and becomes something closer to conversation inside a shared home.

Watch how Paul prays after this shift. In Ephesians 3 he asks the Father to strengthen believers "with power through his Spirit *in your inner being*." The strengthening doesn't arrive from outside like a delivery — it rises from the Spirit already resident within. In Colossians 4 he tells believers to continue steadfastly in prayer, and the whole assumed context is a praying life carried along by an indwelling Helper.

Here's how I've come to summarise the shift: before Pentecost, you prayed *toward* God. After Pentecost, you pray *from within* God's own presence. Same Father. Same reverence. Completely different starting position.

## The Intercessor Moved In

There's one more layer, and it's the one that changed my actual praying.

In John 17, Jesus prays *for* His disciples — for their protection, their sanctification, their unity. It's the most beautiful intercession in Scripture, and it's offered from outside them, on their behalf.

After Pentecost, Paul can write something that would have been unthinkable before it: "the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26).

Read the progression. Jesus prayed *for* them. The Spirit now prays *in* us.

Which means your prayers were never meant to be solo performances. When you kneel down with no words — when all you have is a groan, an ache, a situation too tangled to articulate — the Spirit is already translating. He takes what you can't say and carries it to the Father, aligned with the Father's will, better than you could have phrased it on your most eloquent day.

The pressure to "get prayer right" dies here. You were never the only one praying.

## So Do We Retire the Lord's Prayer?

No — and this matters. The content Jesus taught doesn't expire at Pentecost. "Hallowed be your name." "Give us this day our daily bread." "Forgive us our sins." Every line still stands. What shifts is the *mode*. You're no longer reciting those words up toward a distant Father, hoping they carry. You're praying them in union with the Spirit who already dwells in you — the same requests, now made from inside the relationship they ask for.

A few things that change in practice:

**Prayer becomes less transactional and more relational.** You're not trying to reach God. You're communing with a God who has already reached you.

**Your requests get reshaped.** Notice what Paul prays for after Pentecost — strength in the inner being, open doors for the gospel, knowledge of God's love. The Spirit doesn't just carry your requests; He gradually edits them toward what the Father actually wills.

**Prayer becomes communal.** Acts 2:42 says the church devoted itself to *the prayers* — plural, together. The Spirit doesn't just create praying individuals. He creates a praying people.

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

Are you still praying as if you need to convince God to pay attention — or are you praying as if He's already present, already listening, already translating the groans you can't put into words?

Because only one of those matches the reality you actually live in.
