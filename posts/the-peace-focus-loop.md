## We've Been Chasing Focus Backwards

Every productivity book I've ever picked up treats focus as a discipline problem. Block the apps. Time-box the calendar. White-knuckle your attention into submission.

And for a while, I believed that. Then I started noticing something inconvenient: my most scattered days were never my laziest days. They were my most *anxious* days. The days my mind wouldn't hold still weren't the days I lacked willpower — they were the days I lacked peace.

Which made me wonder if we've had the whole thing backwards. What if focus isn't something you force? What if it's something that grows — and peace is the soil it grows in?

Scripture, it turns out, has been saying this for a very long time.

## A Mind Under Siege Can't Build Anything

Proverbs 17 opens with a line that sounds domestic but cuts deep: "Better is a dry morsel with quiet than a house full of feasting with strife." The writer isn't romanticising poverty. He's making a claim about attention — that turmoil fragments it. When your home, or your inner world, is thick with unresolved conflict, your mind never settles. You're monitoring threats. Replaying arguments. Rehearsing the comeback you should have said three days ago.

Nobody focuses in that state. They can't.

Philippians 4 shows the way out, and the sequence matters. Paul says: don't be anxious about anything — instead, pray about everything, with thanksgiving. And then *the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds.*

That word "guard" stopped me. In the Greek it's *phroureo* — a military term. A sentinel posted at the gate. Paul is saying that peace doesn't just feel nice; it *stands watch*. It forms a perimeter around your thinking so that worry can't keep breaking in and looting your attention.

Without that sentinel, your mind is a city under siege — scanning every horizon, trusting no quiet moment, never resting long enough to build a single thing.

## Then the Loop Turns Around

Here's where it gets beautiful. Isaiah 26:3 — "You keep him in perfect peace whose mind is stayed on you, because he trusts in you."

In Hebrew, "perfect peace" is *shalom shalom*. Peace doubled. Peace compounded. The language itself refuses to say it only once.

And the condition for it? A mind that is "stayed" — the Hebrew *samak*, meaning leaned upon, supported, fixed. The picture is of someone resting their full weight on something that holds.

Read that slowly and you'll notice the flow has reversed. In Philippians, peace made focus possible. In Isaiah, focus — attention steadied on God — is what *produces* the peace.

So it's not a sequence. It's a loop:

- Peace creates the inner space where focus can live.
- Focus, rightly directed, deepens the peace.
- The deeper peace makes the next stretch of focus easier.

Round and round, each one feeding the other. Which also means the loop can spin the other way — anxiety scatters attention, scattered attention breeds more anxiety — and most of us know exactly what that downward spiral feels like by Thursday afternoon.

## The Enemies Never Attacked the Wall

If you want proof that focus is powerful, watch what people do to break it.

Nehemiah was rebuilding Jerusalem's wall, and the work was nearly finished. His enemies had mocked him, threatened him, conspired against him — none of it worked. So they changed tactics. No armies this time. Just an invitation: *come down and meet with us.*

Four separate times they sent it. Four times it sounded reasonable. And four times Nehemiah gave the same answer: "I am doing a great work and I cannot come down."

Sit with how shrewd that attack was. They didn't try to destroy the wall. They tried to destroy his *attention* — because they understood that a distracted builder and a defeated builder end up in the same place.

And notice what distraction looked like. It didn't announce itself as an attack. It dressed up as an opportunity. A meeting. A perfectly reasonable request from people who just wanted a little of his time.

That's still how it works. The things that pull you off your assignment almost never look like enemies. They look like obligations. They arrive wearing the costume of responsibility — which is exactly what makes them so hard to refuse. Not every request for your attention is legitimate, and learning to tell the difference might be one of the most spiritual skills you ever develop.

## Your Feed Is Forming You

A few verses after the sentinel promise, Paul writes what might be the most practical sentence in the New Testament for people who own a smartphone:

"Whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable — think about these things."

Paul isn't being poetic. He's prescribing a content filter for the mind — written two thousand years before the infinite scroll made one necessary.

Because here's the mechanism: what you let into your attention stream shapes your inner state, and your inner state shapes your capacity to focus. Feed yourself a steady diet of outrage, crisis, comparison, and catastrophe, and your internal weather will mirror it. Peace evaporates. Focus scatters. Then you wonder why you can't concentrate, and you reach for the phone again, and the loop spins downward another turn.

But curate deliberately — toward the true, the honourable, the lovely — and you're doing more than protecting your mood. You're training your mind toward both peace and sustained attention at once.

I've started thinking of it this way: curation is a spiritual discipline. What you scroll, what you listen to, what you let replay in your head at night — none of it is neutral. All of it is forming you. The only question is in which direction.

## One More Thing the Loop Needs

There's a piece I almost left out, and it might be the most countercultural one: rest.

The same God who wired peace and focus together also built a rhythm into creation — six days of work, one of rest — and commanded His people to keep it. Not as a reward for finishing, but as a structure for sustaining. Because focus without rest doesn't stay focus for long. It curdles into frantic striving, and frantic striving is just anxiety wearing a work badge.

The Sabbath isn't a pause from the peace-focus loop. It's the maintenance the loop runs on.

So here's where all of this lands for me. If your attention has been scattered lately, the answer probably isn't a better app or a stricter schedule. Start further upstream. Make peace with God about the thing you've been carrying — actually pray about it, with thanksgiving, the way Paul says. Guard the gate of what you consume. Say "I cannot come down" to one illegitimate demand this week. And take a real day of rest without apologising for it.

Then watch what happens to your focus. Not because you forced it — because you finally gave it somewhere peaceful to live.
