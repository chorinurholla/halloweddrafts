## You Were Born Into a Universe That's Already Singing

Here's something I can't shake loose: you have never once reminded the sun to rise. The sea has never asked your permission to roar. Somewhere right now, a tree you'll never see is pushing fruit out of its branches, and it didn't check with anyone first.

Everything around you is doing exactly what it was made to do. And according to Psalm 148, that's not just biology or physics ticking along. That's worship.

Which raises an uncomfortable question — one I've been sitting with for weeks. If the whole universe is already praising, what does it mean that you and I can simply... not?

## The Roll Call Nobody Escapes

Psalm 148 reads like a summons. Not a suggestion — a summons.

It moves in two great waves. The first sweeps the heavens: angels, sun, moon, stars, the waters above the skies. All of them called to praise. The second wave comes down to earth and gets wonderfully specific — sea creatures, fire, hail, snow, storm winds, mountains, fruit trees, wild animals, livestock, reptiles, birds. Then kings. Then princes. Then young men and young women, the old and the young together.

Nobody is left off the list. Nothing is too grand to be summoned and nothing is too small to matter.

And the psalmist gives one reason for all of it: "He commanded and they were created" (v. 5). That's the whole argument. Everything that exists owes its existence to a word God spoke, and the natural posture of a created thing toward its Creator is praise.

Notice what the psalm doesn't ask. It never asks *whether* these things should worship. That's settled. The only live question — and it's the one that will follow us through this whole piece — is what happens when a creature points its worship somewhere else.

## How Exactly Does a Mountain Praise?

This is where it gets strange, and I want to sit in the strangeness rather than rush past it.

What does it look like for hail to worship? For a reptile to exalt the Lord? The psalm doesn't say these things *decide* to praise. It says they *do* praise — and the clue to how is hiding in a sister psalm.

Psalm 19 says the heavens declare the glory of God, that day after day they pour forth speech. Then it makes a point of the paradox: they have no speech, no words, no audible voice. And yet their voice goes out into all the earth.

So creation speaks without speaking. It worships by *being what it was made to be.*

A tree doesn't attend a service. It sends roots down and branches up. It bears fruit in season, gives shade, gives oxygen, gives beauty — and in doing all of that, it's declaring something: *I was made by Someone, and I'm fulfilling His design.* The sea worships by roaring inside its boundaries. The mountain worships by standing where it was placed.

Worship, at its most elemental, is a creature operating in the grain of how God made it. Hold onto that definition. It's about to make human beings look very odd indeed.

## The Only Creature That Can Say No

Here is the asymmetry that stopped me cold.

The sun has never skipped a sunrise out of apathy. The sea creatures swim. The mountains stand. But you? You can watch a sunrise and feel nothing. You can stand in front of the ocean and stay locked inside your phone. You can live an entire life as though you weren't made by anyone, for anything.

Humans are the only part of creation that can withhold worship.

That's not our glory — it's our strangeness. In a universe where everything glorifies God simply by existing, the silent human is the anomaly. The grudging human. The distracted human. We are the one note in the symphony that can choose not to sound.

But the end of Psalm 148 hints at why we were given that terrible freedom. After summoning everything, the psalmist narrows in: God "has raised up a horn for his people... the people of Israel who are near to him" (v. 14). *Near to him.* Creation praises by being what it is, mechanically, beautifully, without a thought. God's people get to praise differently — with consciousness, with gratitude, with choice. We don't just fulfill a design. We *know the Designer*, and we respond to Him personally.

The freedom to refuse is the same freedom that makes love possible. A tree can't adore. You can.

## The Part That Should Scare Us a Little

Psalm 115 takes this somewhere darker, and I don't think we're allowed to look away.

The psalmist describes idols — silver and gold, the work of human hands. Mouths that don't speak. Eyes that don't see. And then one of the most chilling lines in the Psalter: *those who make them become like them* (v. 8).

You become what you worship.

Worship the living God, and you grow more alive — more human, more generous, more secure, because He is those things. Worship dead things — status, money, reputation, the version of yourself you perform online — and something in you goes quiet. Mute like the idol. Blind like the idol.

And here's the twist that took me the longest to accept: you cannot stop worshiping. It isn't on the menu. Your life *will* organise itself around something. Your calendar, your anxiety, your spending, your late-night thoughts — they're already flowing toward some centre. The only question is whether that centre is the One who made you or a substitute that's quietly remaking you in its image.

Refusing God doesn't make you worship-free. It just means you're worshiping below your design spec — a tree trying to grow sideways, a sea creature trying to live on land. You're fighting the grain of your own nature, and the grain always wins eventually.

## So What Do We Actually Do With This?

Three things. None of them are complicated. All of them are uncomfortable.

**1. Audit what your life is praising by default.**

Your calendar is a liturgy. Your screen time is a prayer list. Your bank statement is a tithing record. Don't tell me what matters to you — show me what your days are organised around, and I'll tell you what you worship.

If everything is structured around building the platform, securing the future, managing the reputation — then those things are receiving the attention, energy, and sacrifice that Psalm 148 says belongs to God. You're not failing to worship. You're worshiping the wrong thing, fluently.

**2. Let creation provoke you.**

When a sunrise stops you, when the ocean unsettles you, when you catch a tree bending in the wind — those aren't just pretty moments. They're invitations. Creation is doing what it was made to do, and it's asking you a question: *will you join?* Stop treating the natural world as scenery and start treating it as testimony.

**3. Take seriously that your functional worship is forming you.**

If your real centre is your work, you'll be as hollow as your résumé the day the work is taken away. If it's your image, you'll be as fragile as your last post's engagement. But if the thing your life actually orbits is the God who made you, you'll grow into His likeness — more generous, because He is generous; more secure, because He is sovereign; more creatively alive, because He is the Creator.

One warning before we close, because this is where I see people trip: don't confuse *believing in* God with *worshiping* God. You can hold flawless theology while your life quietly worships comfort, control, or reputation. Worship is not what you say you value. It's what you spend yourself on.

## The Invitation Underneath It All

Here's the reframe that changed how I read this psalm.

You are not being asked to do something strange or unnatural. You're being invited to do the one thing everything else is already doing — the thing you were built to do. The stars are worshiping. The mountains are worshiping. The animals are worshiping. Your silence is the odd thing out in a singing universe.

That's why the entire Psalter ends the way it does — Psalm 150, the finale: "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD." It isn't a command to perform. It's a command to stop fighting your nature. You have breath. That breath is for praise. Every inhale is another chance to finally run on the right fuel.

And here's the part that undoes me every time I think about it. God doesn't need your worship. He is fully God whether you praise or not — so secure in His own majesty that your silence subtracts nothing from Him. But He made you in such a way that you cannot be fully *you* without it. Your worship was never for His benefit. It was always for yours.

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

If a tree worships by being a fruitful tree, and a mountain worships by standing solid where God placed it — what does it look like for *you* to worship by fulfilling *your* design? Not abstractly. Practically. What were you made to do, to build, to steward — and is your life currently structured in a way that lets you do it to God's glory?

Sit with that one. Don't answer it too fast.
