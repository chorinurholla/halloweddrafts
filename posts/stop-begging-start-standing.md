## The Verb Tense That Rewired My Prayers

Sometimes the most important word in a verse is the one doing the quietest work. In 2 Peter 1:3, it's a tense.

"His divine power *has granted* to us all things that pertain to life and godliness."

Has granted. Past tense. Completed action. Not *will grant*, once you've prayed hard enough. Not *may grant*, if your faith clears the bar. The package, Peter says, has already been delivered — everything pertaining to life and godliness, plus "his precious and very great promises," through which we become partakers of the divine nature.

Now hold that verse up against how most of us actually pray. Listen to the average prayer meeting — listen to *me* on an average Tuesday — and you'll hear a lot of pleading with God to release things He has, by His own word, already released. We pray like beggars outside a gate that was opened two thousand years ago.

If Peter is right, then most of us need more than new prayer requests. We need a new prayer *posture* — one that starts from what God has already given rather than from what we're afraid we lack. The old word for this is positional prayer: praying from your position in Christ, not for it.

Let me show you what the shift looks like, because three scenes in Scripture make it concrete.

## Scene One: "What I Do Have"

Acts 3. A man lame from birth sits at the temple gate, asking Peter and John for money. And Peter's reply is one of the great sentences in the New Testament: "Silver and gold have I none — but *what I do have* I give you."

Read that slowly. Peter doesn't lament what's missing. He doesn't pray, "God, if it be Your will, please consider healing this man." He takes inventory of what he actually carries — authority in the name of Jesus — and he deploys it.

That's the move. Prayer rooted in 2 Peter 1:3 is inventory management, not fundraising. You're not raising support from a reluctant donor. You're withdrawing from an account you know is funded — taking stock of what's already been deposited (grace, promises, access, the Spirit of wisdom) and activating it in the specific situation in front of you.

Feel the difference in these two prayers:

*"God, please give me wisdom for this decision. I'm so confused. Help me know what to do."*

*"Father, You've already given me the Spirit of wisdom. I receive that now. Open my eyes to what You're already showing me — I'm not lacking wisdom, I'm learning to recognise it."*

The first treats wisdom as absent. The second treats it as present but unrecognised — which is what James 1:5 actually promises. God "gives generously to all without reproach" when you ask, because the supply was allocated before you asked. The asking isn't persuasion. It's pickup.

## Scene Two: Praying From the Throne Room, Not Toward It

John 4. Jesus, talking with a Samaritan woman, dismantles an entire geography of worship in one sentence: "True worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth." Not on this mountain. Not in that temple. Worship — and prayer is worship — is untethered from location and ritual, and re-tethered to position and reality.

Paul finishes the thought in Ephesians 2:6: God "raised us up with him and *seated us with him* in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus." Seated. Present tense. Already.

Which means something staggering about where your prayers start from. You are not praying *up* toward a distant throne, hoping your voice carries. You are praying *from inside the throne room*, as a co-heir, at a table you were invited to. Hebrews says you can "with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" — not sneak near. Draw near. Boldly. The blood of Jesus is your standing invitation.

Again, feel the difference:

*"God, please be near me. I feel so far from You. Come close."*

*"Father, You've promised You'll never leave me. You're closer than my breath. I'm not asking You to come near — I'm acknowledging You're already here. Tune my heart to recognise Your presence."*

The first treats distance as the problem. The second tells the truth: God hasn't moved. Your attention has. And the entire emotional register of prayer shifts with that one correction — from desperation to declaration, from petition toward participation.

## Scene Three: Fresh Filling, Not New Position

Now, before this becomes a theology of never asking for anything — Acts 4.

Peter and John have just been threatened by the authorities for the healing at the gate. They gather with the believers and pray. And notice what they *don't* pray: "God, make us apostles." They were apostles. Position settled. Instead they pray, "Grant your servants to continue to speak your word with all boldness" — and the room shakes, and they are filled with the Holy Spirit. Again. These are people who were already filled at Pentecost.

This is the nuance that keeps positional prayer honest: your position never changes, but your need for fresh filling never stops. You already have the Spirit; you keep asking to be filled. You already carry authority in Christ; you keep asking for boldness to exercise it. The verb in Ephesians 5:18 is present continuous — *keep being filled* — a standing order, not a one-time event.

So positional prayer doesn't eliminate asking. It clarifies what you're asking *for*. You stop petitioning for a status you already hold and start praying for fresh activation of what that status carries. The apostles didn't need a promotion. They needed courage for Thursday. So do you.

## Rebuilding the Vocabulary

Changing a prayer posture starts with changing sentences. Here's a starter set — catch yourself mid-beg and translate:

**Instead of:** "God, please give me strength, I can't do this." **Stand on:** "Father, Your power is already at work within me. I draw on it now for this specific thing."

**Instead of:** "Lord, please don't abandon me in this." **Stand on:** "You've promised never to leave. So I'm walking into this knowing You're already in the room."

**Instead of:** "God, I beg You to forgive me — again." **Stand on:** "Thank You that in Christ I have redemption, the forgiveness of sins. I confess this honestly and receive what the cross already secured."

**Instead of:** "Please, God, just give me peace." **Stand on:** "You are my peace. I bring You this anxiety by name and let Your peace stand guard over my mind, as You said it would."

One caution before you run with this. Positional prayer is not prosperity magic, and it's not pretending. It doesn't mean every circumstance bends to your declaration, and it never replaces honesty — the Psalms are full of seated co-heirs weeping, lamenting, and wrestling. The position doesn't forbid tears. It just means you cry them *inside* the throne room instead of outside the gate. Confidence in your standing and honesty about your pain are not competitors.

**A question to sit with this week:**

Listen back to your own prayers from the last few days. What percentage of them were begging God for things His Word says He has already granted?

Pick one recurring beg — just one — and rewrite it this week as a prayer from your position. Pray the rewritten version every day. Watch what it does, not to God's willingness, but to your own heart.

The account is funded. Stop pleading at the counter. Make the withdrawal.
