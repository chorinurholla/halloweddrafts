## A Question That Won't Leave Me Alone

It started as a simple chain of thought. God made humanity in His image. And everything God made, He made with staggering care — Genesis keeps repeating it, *and God saw that it was good*, like a craftsman stepping back from the bench after every stage.

So here's the question that's been following me around: if I'm made in the image of a God like *that*, doesn't excellence stop being optional?

Not excellence as perfectionism. Not excellence as hustle-culture grind. Something older and weightier than both: the idea that doing your work well — whatever the work is — might be one of the most basic ways a human being resembles their Maker.

I've come to put it this way: excellence is the proper response to the gift of capacity while we still have it.

Let me show you where that conviction comes from, because it's buried all over Scripture in places we don't usually look.

## The Image Is a Job Description, Not a Portrait

We tend to read "made in His image" as a statement about appearance or dignity — and it is about dignity. But Genesis won't let it stay abstract. The very next breath after "let us make man in our image" is a commission: rule, subdue, fill, be fruitful. Every one of those is a generative verb. Image-bearing isn't a static likeness; it's an active resemblance, expressed through what you make and tend.

And watch *how* God works in Genesis 1, because the pattern is the point.

He speaks — and things exist. He creates in deliberate stages. And after each stage, He stops, looks at what He made, and evaluates it: *God saw that it was good.* Over and over, a rhythm of create, assess, declare. He doesn't move on until He's looked.

That refrain is quality control. The first chapter of the Bible shows us a God who reviews His own work before calling it finished.

So when that God makes creatures "in His image," the implication lands hard: the baseline posture of human work isn't survival, and it isn't efficiency. It's craftsmanship. Phoning it in, cutting corners for no reason, shipping work you never paused to evaluate — these aren't just skill failures. They're identity failures. You were made by a God who steps back from the bench. Refusing to do the same is refusing to image Him.

## About That "Ye Are Gods" Verse

There's a verse people reach for in this conversation, and we should handle it carefully, because it's one of the most misused lines in the Bible.

Psalm 82:6 — "I said, 'You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you.'" Jesus Himself quotes it in John 10. Out of context it sounds like a promotion to divinity. In context, it's almost the opposite — and honestly, the real meaning is more useful.

Psalm 82 is God rebuking corrupt rulers. These were judges meant to carry His justice into the world, and they'd abused the office. Calling them "gods" (*elohim*) is pointed, almost ironic: *you were given God-level responsibility to represent divine justice — and look what you did with it.*

When Jesus picks up the verse in John 10, He's making an argument from the lesser to the greater: if Scripture could call those failed judges "gods" merely because God's word came to them, how much more fitting for the One the Father actually set apart and sent?

So no — the verse doesn't make us divine in essence. It makes us something arguably more sobering: creatures carrying *delegated authority*. Representative function. We're called to exercise God-like stewardship over what's in our hands, not because we are God, but because we are imaging Him in how we handle it.

Which means the question is never "am I divine?" The question is the one Psalm 82 forces: *what are you doing with the authority you were handed?*

## The First Person Filled With the Spirit Was a Craftsman

Here's a piece of Bible trivia that should be far more famous than it is.

The first time Scripture records someone being *filled with the Spirit of God*, it isn't a prophet. It isn't a priest mid-sermon. It's a craftsman named Bezalel, and the filling is for a specific purpose: "ability and intelligence, with knowledge and all craftsmanship, to devise artistic designs, to work in gold, silver, and bronze" (Exodus 31).

God names him personally. Fills him with the Spirit. And sets him to work building the tabernacle — lampstands with almond-blossom detailing, curtains with cherubim woven in, a mercy seat of hammered gold.

Sit with what that establishes: artistic and technical excellence is a legitimate domain of the Spirit's activity. Your design work, your spreadsheet, your code, your strategy document, the event you're coordinating, the meal you're plating — these are not spiritually second-class activities that God tolerates while waiting for you to do "ministry." They are sites where His Spirit is pleased to operate.

And notice what the tabernacle's detail teaches. God didn't ask for a functional tent. Every measurement and material carried meaning — the design itself preached. Which leads to a principle I can't unsee: your work is theology in material form. What you build carries embedded claims about what matters and what kind of world should exist. Sloppy work preaches a sloppy god. Excellence in execution isn't vanity — it's visual apologetics for the kind of God you serve.

## While You Still Have It

Now for the phrase that started all of this: excellence is the proper response to the gift of capacity *while we still have it*.

That last clause is doing serious work.

Genesis 2 says God placed the man in the garden "to work it and keep it" — the Hebrew verbs are *abad*, to serve and cultivate, and *shamar*, to guard. Placement comes with a mandate. If you're somewhere — a role, a season, a market, a body with particular strengths — there's something to build there and something to protect.

But here's what we conveniently forget: the capacities themselves are on a clock. Your energy, your mental sharpness, your creative fluency, your network, your health — none of it is permanent. Plenty of people live as though it were. They defer the hard project, delay the meaningful work, spend their prime years on low-leverage tasks — and wake up decades later wondering where the stamina went.

Capacity is a wasting asset. Failing to steward it with urgency isn't humility. It's ingratitude.

And yet — before this becomes another anxious sermon about maximising yourself — Solomon adds the counterweight. When he built the temple, he demanded the finest cedar, the most skilled craftsmen, gold without compromise. And in the same breath he confessed that the building could never be enough for the God "whom heaven cannot contain."

Both things, held at once. Excellence: pour out your best. Humility: your best is an offering, not a trade. It honours God; it doesn't obligate Him. The craftsman who forgets the first truth gets lazy. The one who forgets the second gets insufferable.

## Three Practices for Monday Morning

So how does image-bearing actually show up at a desk, in a kitchen, on a job site? Three habits, each one borrowed straight from the opening chapters of the Bible.

**1. Evaluate before you ship.** God's rhythm was create, assess, declare — He looked before He moved on. So look. Read the email once more before sending. Ask of the post, the deck, the deliverable: is this good, or is it just *done*? That pause isn't perfectionism. It's the image-bearer's quality control, and it takes thirty seconds.

**2. Name things accurately.** Adam's first recorded act of dominion wasn't building — it was naming. Bringing order through honest categorisation. You exercise the same authority every time you diagnose a problem correctly, label a team dysfunction plainly instead of politely, or describe what your product actually does without hiding behind jargon. Vague language protects dysfunction. The person who names the thing truthfully shapes everything that follows.

**3. Build as worship, not for applause.** Bezalel wasn't building a portfolio. He was building the place where God would dwell — which meant the almond-blossom detail mattered even if no one important ever praised it. Some of your best work will go similarly unnoticed by people. Do it anyway, at full quality. The audience was never the point.

**A question to sit with this week:**

Look at the work currently in your hands — the project, the business, the household, the craft. If someone could only learn about your God by examining *how* you do that work — the care, the honesty, the follow-through — what kind of God would they conclude you serve?

And if the answer stings a little: what's one piece of work you could finish this week the way Genesis finishes a day — paused over, honestly assessed, and only then called good?
