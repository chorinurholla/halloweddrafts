# Borrowed Philosophy — Blog Post

Oct 21, 2026 · @Tolu

## Most of What You Know, You Never Checked

Here's an uncomfortable exercise: pick any strong belief you hold — about success, money, relationships, what makes a life meaningful — and ask yourself one question. *Where did I get this?*

If you're honest, the answer is almost never "I verified it myself." It was handed to you. By parents, teachers, culture, a church, an algorithm, a painful experience you drew a conclusion from at nineteen and never revisited. We like to think of ourselves as independent thinkers, but most of what we "know" is borrowed philosophy — inherited wholesale, installed before we were old enough to inspect it.

Now, that's not automatically a problem. Nobody can rebuild every wheel; inheritance is how knowledge travels. But it carries a quiet risk most of us never price in: we live downstream of other people's assumptions. And if those assumptions contain error, we inherit the error *as if it were truth* — with all the confidence of truth, and none of the verification.

Scripture has a name for where that ends, and it's one of the most quoted and least understood lines in the Bible: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge."

I want to trace how that destruction actually works — because it almost never looks like destruction while it's happening.

## The Diagnostic Trail in Hosea 4

Hosea 4 reads like a prosecutor's opening statement. God files formal charges against His people, and the charge sheet is grim: "there is swearing, lying, murder, stealing, and committing adultery" (v. 2). Street-level moral chaos, everywhere you look.

But watch where the prosecution locates the *cause*. Not in the chaos itself. One verse earlier: "there is no faithfulness or steadfast love, and *no knowledge of God* in the land" (v. 1). And then the famous verdict: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge" (v. 6).

The structure matters enormously. The surface wreckage — the lying, the violence, the betrayal — is the symptom. Eroded foundational knowledge is the disease. The people didn't suddenly become worse people; the orienting knowledge that would have shaped them differently had quietly drained out of the culture, and behavior fractured downstream of the leak.

And here's the detail that should keep leaders and teachers up at night: God traces the leak to the priests. "Because *you* have rejected knowledge, I reject you" (v. 6). The people responsible for transmitting truth stopped transmitting it — so an entire generation inherited a broken system and never knew anything was missing. They operated on borrowed error with complete sincerity.

That's the most chilling part of the whole chapter: the people didn't know they didn't know. You can't fact-check an assumption you've never noticed you're making.

## Error Never Introduces Itself as Error

So if eroded knowledge is the disease, here's the question that matters: how does the erosion happen? How does a person — a family, a culture — trade truth for error without noticing the exchange?

Peter answers it with one devastating adverb. False teachers, he writes, "will *secretly* bring in destructive heresies" (2 Peter 2:1). Secretly. The error doesn't arrive waving a banner. It doesn't announce a hostile takeover. It embeds the deviation inside familiar language — same vocabulary, quietly revised dictionary.

That's the mechanism, and once you see it you see it everywhere. The most dangerous drift never comes from obvious outsiders attacking the walls. It comes from within, from voices using trusted words — *love*, *freedom*, *faith*, *flourishing* — while gradually shifting what the words mean. You keep nodding along because the vocabulary is familiar. By the time the definitions have fully migrated, you're holding beliefs you never consciously adopted.

And this is what makes the knowledge of error so much deadlier than simple ignorance. Ignorance is an empty room — uncomfortable, but honest about itself. Error is a furnished room: it *feels* like knowledge. It presents as wisdom, as progress, as enlightenment. Nobody perishes from believing something that feels like a lie. People perish from believing lies that feel like light.

## The Freedom That Enslaves

Peter goes one step further and names the error's favorite sales pitch: "They promise them freedom, but they themselves are slaves of corruption. For whatever overcomes a person, to that he is enslaved" (2 Peter 2:19).

That's the knowledge of error's signature move in every generation: it markets itself as liberation. Throw off the outdated constraints. Follow your heart. Live your truth. Answer to no one. The pitch is always escape from authority — and the product is always a new master. The person who buys it doesn't feel destroyed. They feel free — right up until they notice they can't stop.

Which hands us a wonderfully practical test, because you don't have to win the philosophical argument to run it. Just check the fruit of the "freedom" being sold: does it produce self-control, or compulsion? Does it make its followers more free — more able to choose, to restrain, to walk away — or less? "Whatever overcomes a person, to that he is enslaved" is an empirical claim. You can observe it.

And underneath the test sits the deeper correction: true freedom was never the absence of all authority. It's submission to the *right* authority — the one who made you and knows what you run on. A fish is not freed by liberation from water. Some constraints are the shape of your flourishing, and the philosophy that teaches you to resent them is not setting you free. It's cutting your moorings.

## The Inventory

So what do you actually do about beliefs you didn't know you borrowed? You audit them. Here's a four-step inventory worth an evening and a notebook:

**1. Name three beliefs you hold strongly.** About anything — work, family, money, suffering, what success means. The ones you'd defend without thinking are the best candidates, because "without thinking" is exactly the condition we're examining.

**2. Trace each one to its source.** Where did you learn it? Can you actually name the person, the experience, the cultural narrative? A parent's fear? A teacher's framework? A wound that drew its own conclusion? If you can't name the source, you're holding unexamined inheritance.

**3. Test it against Scripture — actually test it.** Not "does the Bible mention this topic," but: does Scripture affirm, challenge, or reframe what I believe here? Hold the borrowed belief up to the light and let the text disagree with you if it's going to.

**4. Decide in advance that you'll let it go if it fails.** This is the step that separates an audit from a performance. Testing a belief you've already decided to keep isn't testing — it's ritual.

One more thing about that trail: Paul told Timothy to continue in what he'd learned "knowing *from whom* you learned it." The chain of transmission matters. Borrowed philosophy turns dangerous precisely when you can't name who you borrowed it from or whether their life bore the fruit of it.

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

If it turned out tomorrow that one of your core beliefs — something you've built real decisions on — was borrowed error, would you be willing to release it?

Or has what you "know" become so load-bearing that you'd resist correction even if God Himself offered it?

That's the test of whether your hunger for truth is real — or whether it has a quiet boundary where pride says, *no further.* Find the boundary. That's where the work is.
