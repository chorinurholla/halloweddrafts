# The Degree I Couldn't Put Down — Blog Post

Nov 7, 2026 · @Tolu

## A Confession About a Credential

In the last post I wrote about the sermon that's been rearranging me — the idea that only your Manufacturer knows the truth about you, and that everything else, however loudly or lovingly said, is opinion. I ended by promising to tell you about the label I found on myself when I ran that test. Here it is.

I trained in accounting. Over ten years of my life have gone into that lane — the studying, the credential, the identity that quietly grows around a profession until you stop being someone who *studied* accounting and become "an accountant" in your own head.

And for roughly two years now, that door has not opened. Applications sent, doors knocked, and the lane I trained for has stayed closed. If you've ever stood in a season like that, you know the particular ache of it — not dramatic suffering, just a long, grinding silence where you expected a career to be.

Here's the confession, though, and it's the part that cost me something to see: through all of that, it had genuinely never occurred to me to question whether accounting was the assignment at all. Not once. I treated the question as settled — settled by a certificate. When people close to me suggested I look at managerial roles instead, the thought landed as foreign, almost as disloyalty.

And then the sermon asked its question — *what has the Manufacturer actually said about you?* — and I realized I couldn't point to anything. I had been faithful for ten years to something God never said.

## The Script I Didn't Know I Was Running

When I traced the belief back to its source — the audit I keep writing about on this blog — I found a script, and I found that nobody in Scripture wrote it:

*I trained in accounting → therefore accounting is my calling → therefore I must pursue accounting, whatever the doors say.*

Say it out loud and you can hear what it is. It isn't a promise from God. It's borrowed philosophy — the cultural script that says *your degree determines your destiny*, inherited so early and reinforced so often that it felt like conviction. Nowhere does Scripture say your formal training is God's binding assignment on your life. I had welded a certificate to a calling and then called the weld faithfulness.

And underneath the script sat something even less spiritual: sunk cost. Ten years is a lot to have invested. Walking toward a different door felt like admitting the investment didn't lead where I assumed it would — like "wasting the degree." So I kept knocking on the closed door, and I called the knocking perseverance.

Here's the sentence that finally named it for me: **sunk cost is not a biblical category for discerning vocation.** Scripture asks what God is entrusting to you *now* and whether you're multiplying it — never how much you've already spent on the old assumption. Loyalty to an investment can wear the costume of faithfulness for years. The way you unmask it is to ask one question: *did God actually say this, or did I?*

## What the Manufacturer Had Been Saying All Along

Once the script was on the table, I could finally do what the sermon actually prescribed: stop listening to the script and audit what the Manufacturer had been saying. And here's what unsettled me — He hadn't been silent. I just hadn't been counting any of it as communication.

**The closed doors.** Two years of them. I had filed every one under "testing" — endure, persevere, keep knocking. But God closes doors to redirect, not only to test. Paul and his team were "forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia" (Acts 16:6) — the blocked road *was* the guidance, steering them toward Macedonia. A closed door is data. Two years of them might be a sentence.

**The absence of desire.** If I'm honest — blog-post honest — I don't love the work itself. I never have. Lack of passion isn't a verdict on its own; plenty of callings include unglamorous seasons. But persistent lack of passion, *plus* no fruit, *plus* proven gifting somewhere else? That pattern deserves attention, not suppression.

**The fruit growing in the other field.** Because here's the strange part: while the trained lane stayed shut, I've been successfully running and managing business ventures on the side — operations, people, decisions, stewardship. Fruit, actually growing, in a field I never considered "the real one" because no certificate hangs over it.

**The voices of people who know me.** Family who've watched me work suggested management long before I could hear it. In the last post I argued we shouldn't let "only God's opinion counts" make us unteachable. This was my own test case: their counsel aligned with the fruit, came from the faithful, and produced conviction. That's not noise. That might be the Manufacturer using messengers.

Gifting, doors, desire, counsel — four witnesses, all pointing the same direction, all overruled for two years by one laminated assumption. The parable of the talents never says "be faithful to your training." It says *multiply what you've been given.* And I had been so busy guarding the one talent labeled with my degree that I almost missed the ones actually multiplying in my hands.

## Paul Had More Sunk Cost Than I Do

When the "but I trained for this" objection flares up — and it still does — I've started taking it to Philippians 3, because Paul had more invested in his credential than I will ever have in mine.

"A Hebrew of Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee" (v. 5). That wasn't a degree — it was a lifetime. Elite training under the best teachers, cultural prestige, religious authority, an identity built brick by brick from childhood. If anyone had earned the right to say "I trained as a Pharisee, so I must remain a Pharisee," it was him.

His actual accounting: "Whatever gain I had, I counted as loss for the sake of Christ" (v. 7). And then the posture that undoes the whole sunk-cost instinct: "Forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal of the upward call of God" (vv. 13–14).

Forgetting what lies behind. Not because the training was worthless — God wastes nothing, and Paul's Pharisaic education shows up on every page he wrote. The training got repurposed into the new call; it just stopped being the *map*. Paul let his past equip his future without letting it dictate it.

Jesus put the same principle in one farming image: "No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God" (Luke 9:62). The backward look isn't sentimental — it's navigational. You steer toward what you're staring at. And I had been plowing forward while staring, for two years, at a credential behind me.

## The Question That Split Faith From Fear

I want to be careful here, because there's a version of this story that's just as wrong in the other direction — bailing on every hard season and calling it "redirection." Sometimes closed doors *are* a test. Paul sang in a Philippian jail precisely because God had promised him Rome; the chains were temporary facts passing through a permanent promise. If God has spoken, you stay, and you sing.

So how do you tell the difference between persevering in faith and persisting in stubbornness? For me it came down to one question, prayed with my hands open:

*"Am I loyal to this credential because You told me to be — or because I'm afraid to admit the investment didn't lead where I assumed it would?"*

That question splits the two cleanly. If God has spoken, staying is faith — endure the closed doors like Paul endured the cell. But if God never spoke, and I'm staying to protect a decade-old assumption from embarrassment, that isn't faith. It's fear wearing stewardship's name tag. Faith acts on God's word. Stubbornness acts on my plan. From the outside the two look identical — both look like persistence. Only the question exposes which one you're doing.

I don't have the tidy ending yet. I'm still in the middle of this — praying over doors, paying new attention to the fruit, learning to hold the credential as a foundation rather than a fence. "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding" (Proverbs 3:5) has stopped being a graduation-card verse and started being a daily surrender of one specific assumption. What I can tell you is that the ground feels more honest than it has in two years — because I'm finally asking about the assignment instead of defending the investment.

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

What have you been calling faithfulness that might actually be sunk cost? A career lane, a plan, a version of your future you invested in so heavily that walking away feels like waste?

Take it to the Manufacturer and ask the splitting question: *did You say this — or did I?*

And if He never said it — what are the talents actually multiplying in your hands right now, the ones you've been too loyal to the old assumption to count?
