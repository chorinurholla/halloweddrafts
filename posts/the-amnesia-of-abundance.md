## The Danger Zone Isn't Where You Think

Ask most believers when their faith is most at risk and they'll describe a crisis. The diagnosis. The layoff. The season when the money runs out. We brace for those moments — we assume that's where people lose God.

Moses says the opposite, and he says it to a nation standing on the edge of everything they'd ever prayed for.

Deuteronomy 8 is his warning as Israel prepares to enter the promised land: "Take care lest you forget the LORD your God... lest, when you have eaten and are full and have built good houses and live in them... and your silver and gold is multiplied... then your heart be lifted up, and you forget" (vv. 11–14).

Read the trigger list again. Not famine. Not war. Not exile. *Fullness.* Good houses. Multiplied silver. The danger zone is when everything is working — when the business is profitable, the family is healthy, the plans are landing.

That inverts almost everything we assume about spiritual risk. We keep watch at the gate where trouble enters, while the real breach happens on the side where blessing pours in. Nobody forgets God on the day the car breaks down. We forget Him in year three of things going well — slowly, comfortably, without ever deciding to.

## Why Success Makes God Harder to See

The mechanism is simple once you name it: success obscures dependency.

Think about Israel's two economies. In the wilderness, the supply chain was embarrassingly visible: manna fell every morning, from God, or you starved. It didn't store. You couldn't hoard it. The dependence was experiential — you felt it in your stomach every single day.

Then they cross into the land, and the economics change. Now they're eating from vineyards they didn't plant and drinking from cisterns they didn't dig — but also, increasingly, from fields they *did* plant and businesses they *did* build. You see your effort. You see the harvest. And the line connecting the two runs straight through your own two hands.

God didn't become less involved. He became less *visible* — because His provision started arriving through your competence instead of around it.

That's the modern condition exactly. When you're broke, the answered prayer is unmistakable. When the invoices are being paid on time, provision gets cognitively abstracted — it stops feeling like rescue and starts feeling like revenue. The connection to God's enabling power becomes theoretical instead of experiential. And theory, unlike hunger, is very easy to forget.

## The Most Respectable Lie You'll Ever Tell

Moses names the exact sentence the drift produces: "Beware lest you say in your heart, '*My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth*'" (v. 17).

Notice what kind of lie this is. It isn't rebellion. It isn't blasphemy. It's an attribution error — and like all good errors, it's built mostly out of truth:

"I worked hard for this." True — but incomplete. "I made smart decisions." True — but insufficient. "I built this with my own hands." Visible — but theologically false.

The correction Moses insists on is surgical: "You shall remember the LORD your God, for *it is he who gives you power to get wealth*" (v. 18). Read it carefully — it doesn't say God gives you wealth, though He can. It says He gives you the *power* to get it. The strength. The mental clarity. The relational favour. The market timing. The oxygen in your lungs during the negotiation. Even your capacity to work is a gift — which means there is no version of your success story in which you were the sole author.

Any honest accounting of a significant achievement reveals the same thing: the factors outside your control — the providential introduction, the timing you didn't choose, the break you didn't engineer — did more of the heavy lifting than the factors within it. The self-made narrative survives only by leaving those lines off the ledger.

And our culture prints that narrative on everything. Every "self-made" profile, every hustle-culture caption, every awards speech that thanks the grind — it's Deuteronomy 8:17 with better production values. Even believers end up narrating their own lives in purely naturalistic terms: strategy, effort, networking, timing. God gets edited out of a story He wrote.

## Forgetting Is Not a Memory Problem

Here's where we have to stop treating this as a mild spiritual housekeeping issue. In Scripture, forgetting God is not absent-mindedness. It's functional atheism — living as though He isn't there while still affirming that He is.

Hosea 4:6 shows where it ends: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge... since you have forgotten the law of your God, I also will forget your children." And the destruction Hosea describes isn't lightning from heaven. It's slower and quieter than that:

A generation that doesn't know why it does what it does. Communities held together by pragmatism instead of covenant. Rituals that persist after the knowledge that animated them is gone. Children who know the religion but not the God.

Israel in Hosea's day still had priests, sacrifices, festivals — all the infrastructure, fully staffed. What they'd lost was the memory of the God the infrastructure was built to honour. The form survived. The knowledge didn't. And a form without its knowledge isn't faith on autopilot — it's a building with the load-bearing wall removed, standing only until the next storm.

This is why remembrance in Scripture is never nostalgia. It's covenant fidelity — the active discipline of keeping true what comfort keeps eroding. You can't obey what you don't remember. You can't love what you've forgotten. And what you don't deliberately rehearse, you will eventually, inevitably, lose.

## You Don't Drift Into Remembering

So what's the counter-move? Not trying harder to feel grateful. Moses doesn't prescribe a feeling — he prescribes *architecture*. If amnesia is the default setting of prosperity, remembrance has to be built into your life structurally, the way you'd build a railing before anyone falls.

Four load-bearing practices, each one ancient:

**Give first.** The tithe was never primarily about funding — it was mnemonic. Releasing the first portion trains you, bodily, to remember that the whole hundred percent arrived as gift. Giving from the top of the harvest is an act of accurate attribution.

**Stop weekly.** Sabbath forces you to acknowledge, one day in seven, that productivity is not what holds your world up. The person who cannot stop working has quietly concluded that everything depends on them — which is Deuteronomy 8:17 expressed as a schedule.

**Rehearse out loud.** Regularly tell — in writing, in community, at your table — the specific moments God provided what you could not. Not "God has been good," but dates, amounts, names, the thing that shouldn't have worked out and did. Specificity is what separates testimony from sentiment.

**Keep the gap visible.** Resist automatic lifestyle inflation, so the space between what God provides and what you consume stays wide enough to notice. When consumption rises to swallow every increase, provision becomes invisible again.

None of this is legalism. It's engineering — for a heart whose default is drift. Because the hard truth of Deuteronomy 8 is this: if you don't architect remembrance into your prosperity, amnesia isn't a risk. It's a certainty.

**A question to sit with this week:**

Think of the thing in your life that's currently going well — the stable income, the healthy family, the business that's finally working. Now ask: when did you last thank God for it *specifically*, out loud, the way you once begged Him for it?

If the begging was fervent and the thanking has gone quiet, the drift has already started. The good news is that the correction is the same as it ever was: remember. Deliberately, structurally, this week.
