## The Teacher Who Refuses to Blink

There's a voice in the Bible that modern readers keep mistaking for a cynic. Qoheleth — the Teacher of Ecclesiastes — stares at the one fact every self-help book politely steps around, and he doesn't blink: "The same event happens to the righteous and the wicked... the wise and the fool" (Ecclesiastes 9). Death comes for everyone. The hustler and the coaster. The influencer and the invisible.

And here's the thing — he's not being pessimistic. He's being *accurate*.

Because if that's true, then the whole status economy we exhaust ourselves inside — the perception management, the self-preservation tactics, the energy spent *appearing* successful — is running a race whose finish line erases the standings. Common mortality demolishes every status game. It's the great equaliser, and it exposes what living for reputation actually is: a very elaborate way of spending a life you can't get back.

So the question Ecclesiastes forces is the one most of us are too busy to ask: if the scoreboard gets wiped, what actually matters?

The Teacher's answer surprised me. For a book famous for the word "vanity," the prescription is anything but despair. It's urgency — of a very particular kind.

## The People in Front of You Are the Real Work

The Teacher's first prescription lands in verse 9: "Enjoy life with the wife whom you love, all the days of your vain life that he has given you under the sun."

Don't read "enjoy" as frivolous. In context it's a directive — almost a command. God hands you relationships and assignments *today*, and the fact that life is fleeting is not a reason to coast or despair. It's the reason to engage completely now, because this particular day, with these particular people, will not come back around.

Most of us live the other way. We defer meaningful living to ideal future conditions — I'll be present with my family once this season calms down, I'll invest in friendships after the launch, I'll enjoy my life when it finally looks the way I planned. Ecclesiastes quietly dismantles that whole posture: *now* is the only inventory you actually hold.

Which reframes the people around you. The spouse, the kids, the colleague, the friend going through it — it's so easy to treat them as the background to the real work, the interruptions between important things. The Teacher says it plainly: they're not obstacles to the real work. They *are* the real work.

A diagnostic worth sitting with: are you treating your most important relationships as assumptions, or as assignments?

## Whatever Your Hand Finds to Do

The second prescription, verse 10: "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might, for there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going."

Notice what this verse does *not* say. It doesn't say work hard so people think you're successful. It doesn't mention visibility, platform, or comparison at all. It says: the work in front of you — whatever it is — deserves everything you have, not because it earns you status, but because the brevity of life is an argument for wholeheartedness, never for coasting.

Here's the measuring stick that changes: stop evaluating your work by how it makes you look, and start evaluating it by whether you brought your full self to it. Those are wildly different audits. The first asks, "How did it land?" The second asks, "What did I give?"

And the second one is God's question. Jesus made that unmistakable at the temple treasury, watching the wealthy pour in large gifts — and then a widow drops in two small coins, and He says she gave more than all of them. Why? Because God evaluates sacrifice by personal cost, not by absolute output. A small offering that represents everything outweighs a large gift from surplus.

The same math applies to your work. God doesn't grade on a curve of comparison. The question was never "did you do more than them?" It's "did you give what you had?"

## The Race Is Not to the Swift

Then verse 11 arrives and quietly obliterates the entire performance economy:

"The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor bread to the wise, nor riches to the intelligent, nor favor to those with knowledge — but time and chance happen to them all."

You can be the fastest, smartest, most strategic person in the room and still not win, because timing and providence sit in hands other than yours. Every honest person over thirty has watched this happen — the better product lose, the less qualified candidate get the role, the faithful work go unnoticed while the flashy work gets funded. Ecclesiastes isn't shocked by any of it. It's the normal texture of a world where outcomes were never fully ours.

Which sounds crushing until you see what it frees you from. If outcomes belong to God, then your assignment shrinks to something actually carryable: *effort belongs to you; results belong to Him.* Measure yourself by faithfulness and wholehearted engagement — the things you control — and release the scoreboard you never controlled anyway.

Get that order backwards — take responsibility for results and get casual about effort — and you end up exactly where so many of us live: anxious, controlling, and exhausted, white-knuckling variables that were never in our hands while neglecting the one thing that was.

This is why Scripture's antidote to financial and status anxiety is never "achieve more security." It's "be content with what you have, for He has said, 'I will never leave you nor forsake you.'" Your security was never going to be your achievements. It's His presence.

## Then Jesus Flips the Scoreboard Entirely

Ecclesiastes diagnoses the vanity. The Sermon on the Mount hands down the replacement economy — and it reads like a deliberate inversion of everything our world calls winning.

Listen to who Jesus calls *blessed*: the poor in spirit, not the self-sufficient. The mourning, not the ones pretending everything's fine. The meek, not the aggressive climbers. The hungry for righteousness, not the satisfied. The merciful, not the ruthless. The pure in heart, not the image-managers. The peacemakers, not the empire-builders.

Every single beatitude blesses what the status economy pities. Which means if you're in a season of grief, powerlessness, or aching for things to be made right — Jesus doesn't say "hang in there until you're winning again." He says you're already standing in the kingdom's favored position. The place the world calls losing is where His blessing pools.

And then the line about influence that undoes a thousand personal-brand strategies: "You *are* the salt of the earth. You *are* the light of the world." Not *try to be*. Not *build toward being*. Are. Influence, in Jesus' economy, is a byproduct of living differently — and the moment you start performing it, curating an image of impact, you've become salt that lost its taste. The people most obsessed with looking influential are actively dissolving the thing itself.

So: stop trying to look successful. Live faithfully. The influence takes care of itself — and where it doesn't, it wasn't your assignment.

---

**A question to sit with this week — and fair warning, it's uncomfortable:**

If you woke up tomorrow and learned that no one would ever know about your work, your growth, or your faithfulness — not a soul, not ever — would you still do it?

Whatever just flinched in you when you read that: that's the part still living on the old scoreboard. Bring that part to the God who sees the two coins, measures by cost rather than acclaim, and has never once checked whether you're crushing it.

He's not impressed by the résumé. He's after the wholehearted life. And those, it turns out, are rarely the same project.
