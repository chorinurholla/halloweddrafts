# The Decoder — Blog Post

Oct 22, 2026 · @Tolu

## If Error Wears Truth's Clothing, How Do You Find the Real Thing?

In the last post I argued that most of what we believe is borrowed — and that the knowledge of error never feels like error. It feels like wisdom. It uses truth's vocabulary, markets itself as freedom, and destroys quietly.

Which leaves the obvious and urgent question: if error is that good a mimic, how does anyone ever tell the difference? More information clearly isn't the answer — the most informed people in Hosea's day were the priests, and they were the leak.

Scripture's answer is strange to modern ears, and it comes in three parts: the entry point to truth is a posture, not an IQ. The access to truth is a Person, not a method. And the place that Person does His work is a book He wrote.

Let me walk through all three — because together they form something like a decoder, and without it, you can read everything and still see nothing.

## The Entry Point Is Reverence, Not IQ

Proverbs opens its entire project with a claim that quietly offends the modern mind: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7).

Not access to information. Not intellectual horsepower. Not credentials. *Reverence.* The posture of standing before God as God — that's the doorway, and there isn't another one. Which means a brilliant person without it can accumulate libraries and still be operating inside the knowledge of error, because the fear of the Lord isn't one more fact to learn. It's the lens that lets you see every other fact correctly. Reverence is the soil knowledge grows in; without it, learning is mere accumulation — smart but not wise, informed but never discerning.

And Proverbs 1 adds two details that raise the stakes. First, truth isn't hiding: Wisdom "cries aloud in the street" (v. 20). Publicly available. The problem was never access.

Second — and this is the sobering one — the offer has a shelf life. To those who persistently refuse correction, Wisdom says: "Then they will call upon me, but I will not answer; they will seek me diligently but will not find me" (v. 28). Habitual rejection of correction hardens into an inability to perceive truth even when you finally want it. The faculty atrophies like an unused muscle.

The practical upshot is simple and urgent: stay teachable *before* the storm, not after. The hunger for truth has to be cultivated while your conscience is still soft enough to hear a correction and say, "you might be right."

## The Decoder Is a Person

Then Paul takes it a step further — from posture to access.

"The natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit of God, for they are folly to him, and he is *not able* to understand them because they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14).

Read that carefully, because Paul is making a structural claim, not an insult. He's not saying unbelievers are less intelligent — some of the sharpest minds alive sit outside the faith. He's saying spiritual truth is encrypted to the natural mind. Not hard. *Inaccessible.* "No one comprehends the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God" (v. 11) — and then the stunning gift: "we have received... the Spirit who is from God, that we might understand the things freely given us" (v. 12).

The Spirit is the decoder. The sole access point to God's mind. Which means the sharpest theological intellect without the Spirit is a high-resolution screen with no signal — impressive hardware, nothing coming through.

This also explains something about how God operates that frustrates the self-sufficient: He deliberately hides Himself from the proud and reveals Himself to the dependent. His revelation system bypasses human self-reliance *by design*. You cannot think your way in; you can only be shown.

The working implication: study must travel with dependence. Pray before you read. Approach the text asking, not auditing. Humility isn't the decoration on learning — it's the power cable.

## Where the Decoder Does Its Work

So truth enters through reverence and is unlocked by the Spirit. One question remains: *where* does the Spirit do that unlocking? Mystical impressions? Inner promptings? A feeling at 2 a.m.?

Paul's answer to Timothy is concrete: "All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).

"Breathed out" — *theopneustos* — is an extraordinary word. Scripture isn't merely a book *about* God; it's the product of God's own breath, carrying His life in it. And the same Spirit who reveals truth is the Spirit who authored this text. So the primary place illumination happens isn't in untethered impressions — it's when the Spirit lights up the Scripture He wrote. The Author reading His own book over your shoulder.

And look at the four functions Paul lists, because together they form a complete system for exactly our problem:

- **Teaching** — establishing what's true
- **Reproof** — exposing what's false (this is where borrowed error gets caught)
- **Correction** — the path back when you've drifted
- **Training in righteousness** — staying on the path once you've found it

Diagnosis, exposure, repair, maintenance. Any tool that performs all four is *sufficient* — which is Paul's quiet point. Before bolting another philosophy or methodology onto your life, it's worth asking: have I actually mined what this God-breathed text already provides?

One more safeguard Paul hands Timothy: "Continue in what you have learned... knowing *from whom* you learned it" (v. 14). Timothy could trust his formation because he could name his teachers — a mother and grandmother whose lives bore the fruit of what they taught. The chain of transmission matters. Truth travels through trustworthy hands.

## The Warning: Knowledge Obligates

Before this becomes a celebration of truth-seeking, Scripture adds a weight most of us would rather not carry.

Peter writes that for those who encounter the truth and then walk back into their old patterns, "the last state has become worse for them than the first. For it would have been better for them never to have known the way of righteousness than after knowing it to turn back" (2 Peter 2:20–21).

Sit with that. Knowledge without transformation isn't neutral — it's worse than ignorance. Every truth you encounter raises the bar on your life. Every chapter studied, every sermon heard, every insight that lands: the exposure obligates you. The person who meets truth and returns unchanged isn't back where they started. They're further behind, now with a hardened path where the seed once fell.

Which means the insatiable hunger for truth can't stop at *knowing*. It has to press through to *being shaped*. Otherwise you're not accumulating wisdom — you're accumulating accountability.

And Paul adds the final course-correction, the one that keeps truth-seekers from becoming insufferable: "If anyone imagines that he knows something, he does not yet know as he ought to know. But if anyone loves God, he is *known by God*" (1 Corinthians 8:2–3). Knowledge that makes you feel superior rather than responsible is functioning as pride, not wisdom. The most dangerous person in any community is the one who is right and ruthless about it. The goal was never to know more than others. It's to know Him — and be known.

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

Is your knowledge producing intimacy, or just intellectual certainty?

You can be right about God and still be a stranger to Him — the nine lepers proved it, the Pharisees perfected it. So take one truth you've learned recently — from Scripture, from a sermon, from this post — and ask: has it changed anything I actually *do*?

If not, don't learn another thing this week. Go live that one first. The decoder was never given so we could read more. It was given so we could be remade.
