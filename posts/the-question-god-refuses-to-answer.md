## Which Matters More — What You Do, or How You Do It?

It sounds like the kind of question with an obvious spiritual answer. Surely God cares about the heart, right? It's the thought that counts. How you do a thing matters more than what you do.

I took that question into Scripture expecting to have it settled. Instead I found something more interesting — and more demanding.

God refuses to answer it. Not because Scripture is vague, but because the question itself assumes a split God won't permit. Ask the Bible "which matters more, the what or the how?" and the answer that keeps coming back, book after book, is: *yes*. Both. Together. Inseparably.

And once you see why, it changes how you evaluate your work, your motives, and the gap between the two.

## The Audit Starts Inside

Start with a deathbed scene. David, at the end of his life, is handing the temple project to his son Solomon, and he gives him a warning that has nothing to do with architecture: "the LORD searches all hearts and understands every plan and thought" (1 Chronicles 28:9).

Proverbs 21:2 says the same thing with a merchant's image: "Every way of a man is right in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the heart." Weighs — like goods on a scale. Your résumé goes unweighed. Your motives go on the scale.

So yes: God audits the *how* first. The internal posture, the spirit in which you do the thing, the intention underneath the deliverable. No polished output substitutes for sincerity of intent, and self-assessment is unreliable — every way of a man is right *in his own eyes*.

But don't stop reading at verse 9, because David doesn't. His very next breath to Solomon is: "Be strong and *do it*" (28:20). Build the temple. Actually build it. God examines motive not to replace action but to fuel it — the heart-search exists so that the hands can work with integrity.

The audit starts inside. It never ends there.

## Jesus Drives Every What Down to Its How

The Sermon on the Mount is where this gets surgical.

Six times in Matthew 5, Jesus takes a law — a *what* — and drills beneath it to the *how*. You've heard "do not murder"; I say don't harbour the rage that murder grows from. You've heard "do not commit adultery"; I say deal with the lust that adultery merely completes. The Pharisees had mastered the visible checklist. Jesus exposes the checklist as theatre when the heart behind it is rotting.

It would be easy to walk away thinking Jesus is saying the external doesn't matter — just work on your heart. He closes that door Himself: "I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them" (5:17), speaking of the Law and the Prophets. The commands still stand. The deeds still count.

What He's actually saying is about *direction of flow*. The action flows from the heart, so start at the source — but the river still has to reach the sea. The righteousness that "exceeds the scribes and Pharisees" (5:20) isn't more rules or fewer rules. It's deeper obedience: you don't murder *and* you deal with the anger. You don't commit adultery *and* you guard the thought life. The what and the how, welded.

## The Verse That Holds It All Together

If Scripture has a one-sentence synthesis of this whole question, it's Micah 6:8: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?"

Look at the anatomy of that requirement.

**"Do justice"** is the what — measurable, behavioural, structural. Are your dealings fair? Is anyone being exploited on your watch? Justice isn't a feeling; it's a deed with receipts.

**"Love mercy"** is the how — not merely *showing* mercy but *loving* it. An internal affection for compassion that refuses to weaponise advantage even when you hold all the leverage.

**"Walk humbly with your God"** is the posture underneath both — the standing awareness that Someone sees all of it, the public deed and the private motive alike.

And notice how the three regulate each other. Mercy without justice collapses into passivity — warm feelings while wrong goes unaddressed. Justice without mercy hardens into cruelty — being right at people. Humility is the stabiliser that keeps either from running alone.

One verse. Both halves. Non-negotiable.

## The Two Ways the Split Goes Wrong

Here's why God won't let us rank what and how: pick either one alone and you get a recognizable disease.

**Prioritise the what without the how, and you get Pharisaism.** External compliance, dead heart. The tithe is immaculate and justice goes neglected. The deliverables are impressive and the culture underneath is toxic. The organisation looks great on paper and everyone inside it is quietly bleeding. This is the failure mode of high performers — and the scary part is how long it can pass for faithfulness.

**Prioritise the how without the what, and you get sentimentalism.** Warm intentions, no skin in the game. "I'll pray for you" with full capacity to actually help. "I meant well" standing in the wreckage of what the meaning-well produced. A lot of dysfunction is paved with sincerity that never landed in changed action. This is the failure mode of the well-intentioned — and it feels so virtuous from the inside that it rarely gets diagnosed.

Legalism and sentimentalism are opposite errors with the same root: the belief that God will accept half the package. He won't. What He's after is integrated obedience — the right heart producing the right deed, and the right deed proving the right heart. Jesus put the terrifying version of this in Matthew 7: people who did impressive things in His name hearing "I never knew you." The deeds were real. The relationship wasn't. Half-obedience, in either direction, isn't obedience.

**A question to sit with this week:**

Where are you currently tempted to split the two — excusing a poor process because the output is strong, or excusing missing output because your intentions are good?

Name the specific place. Because integrity, at its root, is just this: the refusal to separate what God has joined.
