# Facts Are Not the Truth — Blog Post

Nov 4, 2026 · @Tolu

## The Sermon Line That Rearranged My Furniture

I sat under a sermon recently that I haven't been able to put down. The preacher's core claim was simple enough to fit on an index card and heavy enough that I've been carrying it for weeks:

*Only your Manufacturer knows the truth about you. Everyone else's view of you is opinion.*

Think about how we treat any manufactured thing. If you want to know what a machine was designed to do, what it can handle, what it runs on — you don't poll the bystanders. You go to the one who made it. Other people can observe the product's performance. Only the maker knows the design intent.

Scripture has been saying this about human beings all along. "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (Jeremiah 1:5). "Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written... the days that were formed for me" (Psalm 139:16). "The LORD sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7).

God's knowledge of you is original. Everyone else's is derivative — assembled from the outside, from partial evidence, filtered through their own lenses. Which means every verdict ever passed on you by a teacher, a boss, a parent, a culture, or your own anxious reflection shares one property: it is commentary from someone who didn't build the machine.

That idea sounds freeing, and it is. But it took me a few weeks of turning it over — and testing it, because every sermon should be tested — to see both what it unlocks and where it needs a guardrail. This post is that working-through.

## When We Lost the Maker, We Started Polling the Bystanders

Here's the part of the sermon that diagnosed something I've watched in my own life: the Fall was, among other things, a catastrophic change of reference point.

Watch the sequence in Genesis 3. Before the Fall, Adam and Eve's identity came from one source — the God who made them and called them very good. After it, everything shifts in a single chapter: they hide from God's voice, severing the connection to the Maker. They feel shame — self-perception now dominated by deficiency instead of design. And they start blaming sideways: Adam points at Eve, Eve points at the serpent. The vertical reference point collapses, and all that's left is horizontal.

And horizontal is where most of us have lived ever since. Having lost contact with the Manufacturer, humanity started measuring itself by the environment — and we've gotten terrifyingly fluent at it:

*Performance:* I'm valuable if I'm succeeding. *Comparison:* I'm acceptable if I'm doing better than them. *Approval:* I'm worthy if they like me. *Credentials:* I matter if I hold the right titles.

Every one of those is a measurement taken from creation instead of the Creator. And every one of them shares the same flaw: the reference point keeps moving. Performance dips. Comparison always finds someone ahead of you. Approval is weather. So identity built on environmental measurement is permanently unstable — not because you're weak, but because you're surveying off a landmark that won't hold still.

Measure yourself by the unchanging Word of the One who made you, and the ground stops moving. Not because your circumstances stabilize — because your reference point does.

## Why Paul Could Sing in a Prison Cell

The sermon's sharpest distinction was between two words we usually treat as synonyms: *facts* and *truth*.

Acts 16: Paul and Silas, beaten and chained in the inner cell of a Philippian prison, singing hymns at midnight. How? Because Paul was holding a word from God — Jesus had said he was a chosen instrument to carry His name "before the Gentiles and kings" (Acts 9:15). Later, in a storm that was tearing the ship apart, an angel repeated it: "Do not be afraid, Paul; you must stand before Caesar" (Acts 27:24).

Kings. Caesar. Not this cell. So the chains were a fact — real, painful, undeniable — but they were not the truth about his life. They were a temporary fact passing through a permanent promise. You can sing in a prison you know you're not dying in.

The preacher gave an image for this that I keep reusing: faith is writing a cheque on a Sunday, when the bank is closed, because you *know* what's deposited — you just can't see the account right now. Faith isn't guessing. It isn't manufacturing optimism. It's acting on known truth you can't currently see. "The assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1).

One honest guardrail, because this idea gets abused: the cheque only clears if the deposit is real. Faith acts on what God has actually promised — not on desires we've laminated with spiritual language. Abraham believed for a son because God *said* son. "I'm believing for the luxury car" is a cheque written on an account nobody opened. The question before any act of faith is simply: did God say this, or did I?

But where He has spoken — over your identity, your forgiveness, your future in Him — the facts of your current chapter are not the final word. Facts are real. Truth is final.

## Free From People — Without Becoming Unteachable

Now follow the thread to its most practical consequence. If only the Manufacturer's verdict is truth, then every other verdict — the insult, the rejection, the label a teacher stuck on you at fourteen, the boss who decided your ceiling — is demoted from *truth* to *opinion*. And opinions don't have the authority to define a product they didn't design.

This is the freedom Jesus names in John 8:32 — "you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" — and it's why Proverbs calls the fear of man a snare (29:25). When you care more about human verdicts than God's, you're trapped in a courtroom where the judges keep changing and the standards shift by the hour. When you know the Manufacturer's verdict, insults still sting, but they stop *sticking*. Rejection still hurts, but it no longer renames you.

But here's where I have to add the guardrail the sermon didn't, because I can feel how this teaching could curdle in me if I let it.

"Everyone else's view is opinion" must not become "no one can correct me." Scripture won't allow that move. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend" (Proverbs 27:6). "The ear that listens to life-giving reproof will dwell among the wise" (Proverbs 15:31). Paul — the same Paul who answered to no human court — publicly corrected Peter, and Peter took it. God's verdict is ultimate, yes. But God frequently *delivers* His verdict through faithful people who can see your blind spots precisely because they're standing outside you.

So the test isn't "is this from God or from a person?" It's: does this person's word align with Scripture, come from someone walking faithfully, and produce conviction rather than mere shame? If so, receive it — that may be the Manufacturer speaking through a messenger. Freedom from the fear of man was never meant to be isolation from the wisdom of the faithful. One is security. The other is pride with a Bible verse on it.

## Test Everything — Including This

The sermon ended with an instruction that doubled back on itself, and I respected it enormously: *test every teacher against Scripture — including me.*

That's the Berean posture. When Paul preached in Berea, the listeners "received the word with all eagerness, *examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so*" (Acts 17:11) — and Luke calls them noble for it. They fact-checked an apostle against the manual, and heaven applauded. "Let God be true though every one were a liar" (Romans 3:4).

And then the preacher said something I've been chewing on ever since, about his own upbringing: *"It was true that they taught me those things. But what they taught me was not truth."*

Read that twice. It's a fact that you were taught it — by sincere parents, trusted teachers, a church you loved. The sincerity is real. The transmission happened. But sincerity doesn't make a teaching true; alignment with God's Word does. "There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death" (Proverbs 14:12). You can be sincerely, generationally, culturally wrong — and never know it, because everyone around you inherited the same error.

Which is why this whole framework ends where it began: at the manual. Go to the source. Check the labels you've been wearing — the ones handed to you by teachers, bosses, parents, culture, your own worst seasons — against what the Manufacturer actually wrote. Keep what He confirms. Return what He never said.

**A question to sit with this week:**

What's one verdict about yourself that you've been treating as truth — when it's actually just a fact that someone said it?

Name who said it. Then open the manual and check whether the Manufacturer ever did.
