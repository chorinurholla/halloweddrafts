## One Word, Eighteen Months Ago

About a year and a half ago, I heard a single word in a dream.

*Teleprompter.*

No context. No scene I can remember. No voice explaining it. Just the word — specific enough that I couldn't dismiss it, strange enough that I couldn't use it. I woke up, turned it over, and found nothing to do with it. I don't work in television. I wasn't making videos. It sat there like a key to a door I couldn't find.

So I did the only thing I knew to do with the pull I'd been feeling toward Scripture: I kept writing. For months I've been studying the Bible and documenting what I find — principles, patterns, things in the text I'd never been shown. The writing became a discipline, then a love. But the word from the dream stayed unexplained, filed away in the drawer where you keep the things God says that don't make sense yet.

I'm writing this post because the word finally made sense — and because of what had to be dismantled in me before it could.

## The Objection I Couldn't Argue Down

Here's what I haven't said yet, and what this whole story turns on: I own and operate a nightclub.

Sit with that next to everything else I've told you. A man who runs a nightclub, feeling an undeniable pull to share the Word of God publicly. For a long time, every time the pull got strong, the same voice met it at the door:

*How will you, of all people, stand up in public and share the gospel? Won't it look like a joke?*

And I want to be honest about how reasonable that voice sounded. It didn't feel like temptation. It felt like humility. Like self-awareness. Like protecting the gospel from the embarrassment of being carried by someone like me. Surely the message deserves a cleaner messenger — someone whose weekdays match their Sundays, whose business card wouldn't make the congregation raise an eyebrow.

So I kept the studying mostly private and the writing quiet, and I told myself I was being prudent. Waiting until the résumé was tidier. Waiting until the tension between what I do and what I felt called to say had resolved itself.

I know now what that waiting actually was. But it took a Sunday service and a two-thousand-year-old conversation at a well to show me.

## The Woman Jesus Went Out of His Way to Find

Recently, in church, we landed on John 4 — Jesus and the Samaritan woman. I've read that chapter before. I've probably skimmed past it a dozen times. This time it read me.

Look at who this woman was, by every standard her world used. The wrong ethnicity — a Samaritan, in a day when Jews travelled *around* Samaria rather than through it. The wrong history — five husbands, and the man she was living with was not her husband. The wrong theology — worshiping on the wrong mountain, by Jerusalem's reckoning. Even the wrong social standing among her own people — she came to draw water alone, at noon, in the heat, when the other women came together in the cool of the morning. An outcast's schedule. You keep those hours when you're avoiding people.

And Jesus went through Samaria to meet her. Spoke to her first. Told her everything she ever did — all five husbands, the current arrangement, the whole hidden résumé — and here is the detail that undid me: *He didn't condemn her.* No lecture. No probation period. No "sort your life out and come back." He saw all of it and offered her living water in the same breath, and then revealed Himself to her — "I who speak to you am he" — before the religious establishment heard anything of the kind.

She left her water jar — the thing she came for — and ran back to the very village she'd been avoiding. And her message wasn't polished. It wasn't "I've sorted out my theology; listen to me." It was: "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did."

*All that I ever did.* She led with the thing she had every reason to hide.

## Verse 39 Is Where It Broke Open for Me

Then comes the verse that was aimed, I'm now convinced, directly at the objection I'd been nursing for months:

"Many Samaritans from that city believed in him *because of the woman's testimony*" (John 4:39).

Because of *her* testimony. Not a rabbi's. Not a priest's. Not someone with a defensible reputation. The first evangelist to that city — one of the first in John's whole Gospel — was the woman with five husbands and an outcast's water schedule. The town believed because of the testimony of the person the town had written off.

And I think I can see why it worked. She had nothing to protect — no reputation left to manage, so her words carried no performance. Her transformation was visible — the woman who avoided the village at noon was now running through it, speaking to everyone. And she pointed past herself — not "look how I've changed," but "come and see *Him*."

Here's what that did to my objection. I had been assuming my credibility to speak about God depended on my résumé being clean first — that the nightclub disqualified me until it was behind me. John 4 says credibility doesn't come from a tidy history. It comes from an honest encounter. The woman didn't testify after her transformation was complete and respectable. She testified the same afternoon, mid-mess, jar still sitting at the well.

You don't testify after the transformation. You testify *during* it. The tension I'd been treating as the disqualification — a man between the world he's in and the calling he hears — might be the very thing that makes the testimony believable to people standing in tensions of their own. The people who'd dismiss a nightclub owner quoting Scripture were never going to listen anyway. But the people caught where I'm caught — between the life they're living and the life they suspect God is calling them to, convinced they're too compromised to start — those people don't need a cleaner messenger. They need an honest one.

## And Then the Word Made Sense

Which brings me back to the drawer where I'd filed that one strange word.

A teleprompter, if you've never thought about it, is the tool that lets someone deliver a prepared message straight into a camera while keeping their eyes on the person watching. It's what makes spoken words feel like conversation instead of recitation. It is, almost too precisely, a tool for *saying out loud, face to face, what you have already written down.*

I have been writing for months. Studying, documenting, filling a blog with what I find in Scripture. And lately I've felt the limitation of it: the generation I most want to reach doesn't sit with long-form text. They watch. They listen. A written page asks for attention they've been trained out of giving — but a face, speaking honestly into a camera, still gets through.

Eighteen months ago, I heard a word for a tool I had no use for. Today, I have a library of writing and no voice attached to it. The word wasn't random. It was early — the vision arriving before the preparation it was waiting on. The writing wasn't the destination; it was the script library. And the thing that had to break before I could pick up the tool wasn't technical. It was the lie that someone like me shouldn't be seen saying these things.

So this post is me leaving the water jar. I don't have it all figured out — the tension between what I run and what I'm called to say is still real, and I expect to work it out in public, in real time, the way the woman at the well did: mid-story, not after it. But I'm done waiting for a cleaner résumé before I open my mouth. The testimony is "come and see" — not "look at me."

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

Is there a word you've been carrying — a prompting, a dream, a pull you've filed away as unexplained — that's been waiting not for more clarity, but for more obedience?

And if you've been silent because of who you are or what you do — because the testimony would come from someone with your history — read John 4:39 again. The village believed *because* of her story. Not despite it.

Maybe yours works the same way.
