## The Strangest Instruction at the Jordan

Israel has just crossed the Jordan River on dry ground — the second water miracle in a generation, the moment the promise finally becomes soil under their feet. You'd expect the next command to be tactical: fortify, advance, prepare for Jericho.

Instead, God's first instruction on the far bank is this: go back into the riverbed and carry out twelve stones. Stack them where you camp tonight (Joshua 4:1–9).

A pile of rocks. That's the move. Before the first battle, before a single city falls, God commissions a monument.

And His stated reason is even stranger than the instruction: "When your children ask their fathers in times to come, '*What do these stones mean?*' then you shall let your children know: Israel passed over this Jordan on dry ground" (vv. 21–22).

[In the last post](/posts/the-amnesia-of-abundance) I argued that forgetting is the default — that prosperity quietly erases the memory of the God who provided it. This is God's counter-engineering, and it's worth studying closely, because the design principle inside it runs all the way from a riverbed in Canaan to the bread and cup on a communion table.

## Monuments Don't Preserve Memory. They Provoke Questions.

Here's the genius of the design, and it's easy to miss: God doesn't say "remember this moment." He knows better. Feelings fade. The adults who stood at the Jordan's edge will forget the exact awe within a decade, and their children weren't there at all — they have no firsthand memory to preserve.

So the stones don't store the memory. They *provoke the question that lets the memory be transmitted.*

Picture the mechanism working. Decades later, a child walks past the pile and asks, "Dad, why are these rocks here?" And the father gets to say: "Let me tell you what God did." The monument doesn't do the remembering — it creates the conditions for the conversation. It's a question machine, deliberately installed in the landscape so that the story *has* to be told again.

That's the principle: memory fades, monuments endure — and a monument's job is to make no sense without the story. If the stones could be explained by geology, they'd fail. It's precisely their strangeness — twelve river stones stacked on dry land — that forces the question.

Notice too what this means about whose job remembrance is. The stones ensure the question gets asked. But if the father never tells the story, they're just rocks. Transmission takes both: the artifact that provokes, and the person willing to answer with specifics — not "God is faithful," but "the water stood in a heap, and we walked across on dry ground, and I was there."

## Communion Is Christianity's Pile of Stones

Now jump forward to a Thursday night in Jerusalem. Jesus, on the night He was betrayed, doesn't just say "remember Me." He does exactly what God did at the Jordan — He institutes an artifact:

"This is my body... this is my blood... *do this* in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19–20).

Bread and wine are the stones in the riverbed. Physical elements that make no sense without the story. Every time the church takes them, the elements provoke the question — *why do we do this?* — and the answer rehearses the entire gospel: His body was broken. His blood was shed. He did this for me. I did not save myself.

And Jesus commands the ritual for the same reason Moses commanded remembrance: He knows what's coming. The church will be delivered, then grow, then prosper — and prosperity will do to the church exactly what it did to Israel. The cross will start to feel like background. Grace will quietly turn back into performance. "Christ died for me" will erode into "my effort keeps me here" — which is just "my power and the might of my hand" wearing church clothes.

So He builds the barrier in advance: a meal you cannot take without telling the story again. Paul makes the proclamation explicit — "as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you *proclaim the Lord's death* until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26). Communion is past-facing and future-facing at once: He died, and He is coming back. The table keeps you tethered between the two.

Which reframes what's happening next time the tray passes down your row. It isn't a somber add-on to the service. It's the oldest memory technology the church possesses — a monument you eat — and it exists because Jesus knew you would forget even the most dramatic rescue in history unless He made you rehearse it.

## One Generation From Conquest to Apostasy

If you want to know what happens when the stones stand but nobody tells the story, Scripture runs the experiment for us — and the results are brutal.

Judges 2:10, one generation after Joshua: "There arose another generation after them *who did not know the LORD or the work that he had done for Israel.*" The very next verses: they serve the Baals. The children of the people who walked through the Jordan on dry ground are worshiping the gods of the neighbours.

One generation. From conquest to apostasy. The monuments were presumably still standing — but somewhere between Joshua's death and Judges 2, the transmission failed. Either the children stopped asking, or the parents stopped answering. Either way, the story died, and the faith died with it.

And the drift usually isn't that fast. Watch how it typically runs across a family, a church, or an institution:

**Generation one** says: "We were rescued by grace, and everything flows from that." The story is central and told constantly.

**Generation two** says: "We believe what our parents believed, and we keep the practices they taught us." The story is assumed, not rehearsed.

**Generation three** says: "We're the people who do these practices. That's who we are." Identity has detached from the story entirely.

**Generation four** asks: "Why do we do any of this?" — and nobody can answer. So they leave, or they keep the shell for culture's sake, which is its own kind of leaving.

This is Hosea's "destroyed for lack of knowledge" in slow motion — and it's why you are always one generation away from losing the gospel. Not through persecution. Through silence. Transmission is not automatic; it is the deliberate, repeated, story-driven work that every generation must do or no generation after it can.

## Build Your Own Pile of Stones

So the application isn't abstract. If monuments provoke the questions that keep the story alive, then build some. A few that work:

**Keep physical artifacts.** A handwritten journal of answered prayers, with dates. The framed contract from the deal that shouldn't have closed. The photo from the season you barely survived. A saved voicemail from the person who spoke life when you were ready to quit. Physical objects outlast emotion — they sit on the shelf and provoke the memory just by existing. Don't trust your feelings to carry the story; feelings are the first thing to fade.

**Write the specifics down while they're fresh.** Not "God provided" — but what you had, what you needed, when it arrived, and why you couldn't have engineered it. In five years, the vague version will be unfalsifiable and unmoving. The specific version will still preach — to others, and to you on the day you're low again.

**Live in a way that makes people ask.** This is the quiet one. When you forgive what others would avenge, give under financial pressure, rest when the market says hustle — you become the stones. The question "why did you do that?" is the modern child at the riverbank, and it's your opening to tell the story. Not a principle. A story: "Let me tell you about the time God provided when we had almost nothing in the account and the car died the same week."

**Tell stories, not abstractions — especially to your children.** Children forget principles and remember narratives. "God is faithful" evaporates; "here's the specific prayer I prayed, on this date, and here's exactly how it was answered" lodges for life. And let them see you repent, out loud, when you wrong them — nothing transmits the gospel like watching an adult need it.

**A question to sit with this week:**

If someone walked through your life — your home, your habits, your calendar — what would make them stop and ask a question? Is there any monument standing anywhere? Anything that makes no sense without the story of what God has done?

If the answer is nothing — that's this week's assignment. Pick one moment of God's faithfulness you never want your household to lose, and build its stone: write it, frame it, keep it where it will be seen.

Then wait for someone to ask. That's what it's for.
