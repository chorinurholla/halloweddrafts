## Where the Split Actually Shows Up

[In the last post](/posts/the-question-god-refuses-to-answer) I argued that God refuses to rank *what* you do against *how* you do it — that He demands both, welded, and that splitting them produces either Pharisaism (polished performance, dead heart) or sentimentalism (warm intentions, no follow-through).

That's the theology. But theology you can't locate in an ordinary week is just decoration.

So here are four scenes from Scripture where the what/how split gets tested in real time — a roadside, a kitchen, a hiring decision, and a finish line. Between them, they cover most of the places you and I will face the same test this month.

## Test One: The Roadside

The parable of the Good Samaritan has been sanded smooth by familiarity, so recover the scandal in it.

The priest and the Levite who passed the bleeding man had *defensible reasons*. Touching what might be a corpse would render them ritually unclean — disqualified from temple service. Their what was technically correct. Their theology checked out. And Jesus tells the story so that their correctness condemns them, because the posture underneath it — institutional propriety outranking a human being bleeding in the dirt — was rotten.

The Samaritan had no obligation and every cultural reason to keep walking. But the text says "he had compassion" — the how — and then itemises the what: he stopped, he touched, he bandaged, he paid, he came back. Jesus doesn't ask which traveller had the right doctrine. He asks, "Which of these three *proved* to be a neighbor?" Proved. The deed is the evidence; the compassion is what made the deed possible. Neither alone would have saved that man.

Here's the sentence from this parable I can't shake: proximity defines responsibility. Your neighbour is not the person you feel comfortable helping. It's the person in front of you who needs help. When someone's need lands in your path and you have capacity, the question is no longer "is this technically my job?" It's "am I willing to stop, touch, and pay?"

## Test Two: The Kitchen

The very same chapter of Luke swings the test around to face the busy and the devout.

Martha is doing good work — hosting Jesus, preparing the meal, serving. Nothing on her task list is wrong. But Luke's diagnosis is precise: she is "distracted with much serving" and "anxious and troubled about many things." The what is service to Jesus. The how has curdled into anxiety — and resentment, because she's now demanding that Jesus conscript her sister into her stress.

Mary, meanwhile, is doing one thing: sitting at His feet, listening. And Jesus says Mary chose "the good portion."

Read carefully, this isn't a parable against hard work. It's a warning about a specific and devastating irony: the work you do *for* God can become the very thing that keeps you *from* God. Productivity is not the highest virtue. Presence is. And service that loses its tether to presence doesn't just exhaust you — it turns self-justifying, measuring everyone else by its own anxiety.

If you lead anything, build anything, or carry a relentless season, this test has your name on it. The output demands are real. But if the how of your interior life — the prayerfulness, the capacity to sit down and listen — erodes in the name of the what you're producing, you are building impressively on sand. The one necessary thing was never the meal prep.

## Test Three: The Hire

Nehemiah has just finished rebuilding Jerusalem's wall — one of the great project-management feats in Scripture — and now he has to appoint someone to run the citadel. His selection criterion is recorded, and it should unsettle every hiring manager who reads it.

He chooses Hananiah, "for he was a more faithful and God-fearing man than many" (Nehemiah 7:2).

Not "more experienced than many." Not "the strongest portfolio." *More faithful and God-fearing.* Character was the qualifying credential. Competence still mattered — Nehemiah was no romantic about skill, and he didn't hand the citadel to someone incapable. But between the capable, the tiebreaker wasn't talent. It was trustworthiness under pressure.

The principle for anyone who hires, promotes, or delegates: gifting without character eventually endangers the very thing it was hired to lead. The how someone operates when no one is watching is more predictive than the what they deliver when everyone is. Résumés describe the what. References, if you ask the right questions, describe the how. Weight them accordingly — because you can train skill into a faithful person far more easily than you can train faithfulness into a skilled one.

## Test Four: The Finish Line

The last test is the longest one — it takes a whole life to grade.

Paul, writing his final letter from a Roman prison, sums up his course in three past-tense verbs: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith" (2 Timothy 4:7).

Look at the mix. *Fought* and *finished* are whats — measurable engagement, an assignment carried all the way across the line. *Kept the faith* is a how — internal fidelity maintained through desertion, hardship, and loneliness. He names both, because at the finish line neither can stand in for the other.

And notice what Paul doesn't say. Not "I had good intentions." Not "I stayed busy." But also not "I built the biggest ministries" or "I posted the best numbers." The what he measures is faithfulness to *his* assignment — not comparison to anyone else's.

That's the final shape of the integrated life. When you're at the end looking back, the question won't be "did I do impressive things?" It will be: did I finish what I was given, and did I keep faith while finishing it?

---

**A question to sit with this week:**

Four tests — the roadside, the kitchen, the hire, the finish line. Which one is live in your life right now?

Is there a need in your path you've been evaluating instead of answering? A good work that's quietly replaced your attention to God? A decision where you're weighing talent above trustworthiness? A long assignment you're tempted to finish impressively rather than faithfully?

Pick the live one. That's where the integration gets built — not in theory, but this week, in that exact spot.
