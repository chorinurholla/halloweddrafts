## Let's Talk About the Elephant in the Prayer Room

If you've spent any time in charismatic circles, you've felt the pressure. Somebody says "pray in the Spirit," and what they mean — what everyone understands them to mean — is *pray in tongues*. And if you don't, there's an unspoken question hanging in the air about whether your prayers have full access.

I understand the logic. If the Spirit is now the one who carries our prayers — if He's the access point — then it seems to follow that praying in a Spirit-given language must be the premium channel. For a while, that reasoning had me half-convinced that tongues wasn't just a gift but a requirement.

So I went looking at what the texts actually say. And the answer is clearer than the debate suggests: no, praying in the Spirit does not mean praying in tongues. They are not synonyms. One is a universal command given to every believer. The other is a specific gift that Scripture plainly says not everyone receives.

Let me show you how I got there — because the trail matters more than the conclusion.

## Start With What the Verse Actually Says

The proof text is Ephesians 6:18 — "praying at all times in the Spirit, with all prayer and supplication." It comes at the climax of the armour-of-God passage, the posture in which the whole battle is fought.

Two observations unravel the tongues-only reading.

First: "with *all* prayer and supplication." The Greek *pasēs* means every kind. Paul is deliberately widening the category, not narrowing it. Petition, intercession, thanksgiving, confession, lament, worship — Spirit-enabled prayer comes in every form prayer comes in. If Paul meant one specific form, "all prayer" is a strange way to say it.

Second: when Paul wants to talk about tongues, he says tongues. He has a word for it — *glōssais* — and he uses it freely in 1 Corinthians 14. Here he doesn't. And throughout Ephesians, "in the Spirit" consistently means Spirit-enabled, not ecstatic: believers are built into a dwelling place for God "in the Spirit" (2:22), the mystery of Christ is revealed "by the Spirit" (3:5), and being "filled with the Spirit" (5:18) gets defined as worship, gratitude, and Scripture-soaked community. Not once does the phrase mean an unknown language.

The context seals it. Ephesians 6 is spiritual warfare — "we do not wrestle against flesh and blood." Praying "in the Spirit" there means you cannot fight invisible enemies on human strength. It's a statement about the power source, not the vocabulary.

## Paul Settles It Himself

If there's any lingering doubt, 1 Corinthians 12 ends it. Paul runs through the gifts — wisdom, healing, prophecy, tongues, interpretation — and says the Spirit "apportions to each one individually as he wills." Then he asks a string of rhetorical questions: Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Do all work miracles? *Do all speak with tongues?*

The expected answer, every time, is no.

Now hold that next to Ephesians 6:18, which commands *every* believer to pray in the Spirit at all times. If praying in the Spirit required tongues, Paul would be commanding all Christians to do something he explicitly says not all Christians can do. The command is universal; the gift is not. So the command cannot mean the gift.

And here's what I love about Paul on this: he's no sceptic. "I thank God that I speak in tongues more than all of you," he tells the Corinthians. He prays in tongues *and* he prays with his mind — "I will pray with my spirit, but I will pray with my mind also" — and he refuses to rank one above the other as the real thing.

Tongues is one expression of Spirit-enabled prayer, real and valuable for those who have it. It is not the definition of Spirit-enabled prayer. The umbrella is bigger than any one gift underneath it.

## Why This Matters More Than a Doctrinal Quibble

Maybe you're thinking: fine, it's a technicality. It isn't. Follow the tongues-only reading to its conclusions and watch what breaks.

If praying in the Spirit requires tongues, then every believer without that gift is locked out of spiritual warfare — Ephesians 6 becomes a battle plan most of the army can't execute. Paul's own recorded prayers — Ephesians 1, Ephesians 3, Colossians 1, all in ordinary Greek, all theologically rich and fully intelligible — would violate his own command. And Jesus Himself, anointed with the Spirit without measure, never prays in tongues anywhere in the Gospels. John 17, the most Spirit-saturated prayer ever prayed, is in plain language from the first word to the last.

A definition of Spirit-prayer that disqualifies Paul's prayers and Jesus' prayers is not a definition. It's a mistake.

And pastorally, the mistake has teeth. Make tongues the litmus test and you build a two-tier Christianity — the believers with "real access" and everyone else. You pressure sincere people into manufactured experience, faking syllables to belong. And you shift everyone's attention from the reliable evidence of the Spirit's filling — love, joy, peace, patience, the slow transformation of character — onto a single manifestation that was never meant to carry that weight.

The Spirit distributes gifts as He wills, Paul says, precisely so that no one gift becomes the membership card.

## So What Does Praying in the Spirit Actually Look Like?

Strip away the controversy and a beautiful picture emerges. Praying in the Spirit means praying:

**In dependence, not self-reliance.** Every Spirit-prayer starts, spoken or not, with "I can't do this on my own strength." You're not performing; you're leaning.

**Grounded in Scripture.** The Spirit inspired the Word, so praying the Word back to God is praying in perfect alignment with Him. Daniel's great prayer in Daniel 9 began because he was reading Jeremiah — the text drove him to his knees. Open your Bible, find a promise, and pray it back.

**With watchfulness.** "Keep alert with all perseverance," Paul says. Spirit-prayer pays attention — to what God is doing, where doors are opening, where the enemy is pressing. It's not reciting a list; it's praying with your eyes open.

**For other people.** The command in Ephesians 6:18 ends with "making supplication for all the saints." Spirit-filled prayer keeps turning outward. Who's under pressure right now? Name them.

**Trusting the Translator.** When you don't know what to pray — and some seasons you won't — the Spirit "intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). Your incoherence is not a barrier. It's raw material He knows how to carry.

And yes — if the Spirit has given you tongues, pray in tongues. Gladly, gratefully, privately and freely. Just don't hand anyone else your gift as their requirement.

The access point was never a technique. It's the Spirit Himself — and He already lives in you.

If you've been taught that "real" prayer requires a particular form — tongues, eloquence, length, intensity — what changes when you realise God values your plain-language, Scripture-rooted, utterly dependent prayers just as much?

What would you finally pray about if you stopped worrying about praying it correctly?
